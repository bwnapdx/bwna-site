Technology encourages the concept of multitasking, which attempts to help juggle multiple thoughts and actions at the same time. For example taking a phone call while checking e-mail while simultaneously reviewing documents. Psychologists who study brain function and information storage and retrieval describe the brain as a massive associative network of interconnected thoughts that light up in the brain as needed to create the illusion of multitasking. Unfortunately, there are inherent limits to how many parts of the brain can be lit up at the same time. In studies, individuals asked to perform excessive, laboratory-induced multitasking, showed increase tension, diminished perceived control and even physical discomfort. They became nervous, anxious, and eventually became withdrawn or even frenzied (Monet & Lazarus 1991). One of the most pressing problems associated with technostress was that people do not get to unwind. There is a continual, unrelieved build up of stress on a chemical level which in turn eventually contributes to a behavioral and/or physical problem.

Symptoms of technological stressors are:

- Feelings of memory loss, forgetting what you started to do or why you walked into a particular room
- Impatient with self and others
- A lessened ability to relax or slow down
- Anxiety over having access to your cell phone, palm, TV or other types of techno equipment
- Headaches, stomach discomfort, back pain, ulcers, and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
- Difficulty falling asleep
- Continuously checking your e-mail, voicemail, surfing the web, and other types of techno equipment, and not turning it off for the night

To manage the high-tech age of overload, we must go to the place it originates, the brain. To help the brain retain cognitive fitness, we must look at factors that affect the brain, primarily Glucose (sugar) and H2O (water). Low levels of glucose (hypoglycemia) and dehydration significantly affect the functioning of the brain. Eating complex carbohydrates and fresh fruit and vegetables is a great way to replenish glucose and not feel run-down. The slow release of glucose from complex carbohydrates keeps the brains energy level consistent with less dramatic spikes representing a healthier choice then that of simple carbohydrates like fruit juice, candy, or sweet treats which break down immediately causing the body to have dramatic spikes. Here are some tips to manage technostress in your life:

- Staying hydrated
- Give yourself more time to do everything by building a margin for error
- Schedule time to take care of specific daily tasks
- Set regular “Time-Out” periods or simply go outside and take a break
- Stretch, exercise, and do some deep breathing
- Take a short vision break every 30 minutes
- Limit multitasking
- Plan ahead if possible
- Resist the urge to acquire new technology and use technology to publicize your boundaries
- Educate and contract with others realistic time lines

Technology is here to stay. It is a permanent brick in the wall of our society, and it is still in its infancy and we are just learning its effects on our mind and bodies. It will only grow in use and with it the human state of technostress.
