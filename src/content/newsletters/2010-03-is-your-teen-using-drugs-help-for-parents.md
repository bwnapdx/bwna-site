“I know my son is using drugs, but he refuses to get help. What can I do?” This is a common dilemma faced by thousands of families in both urban and rural areas and across all socioeconomic levels. It’s been estimated that in the U.S. the vast majority of adolescents who are in trouble with drugs don’t get the treatment they need. Some are eventually coerced into treatment by the legal system or school requirements. However, most continue to fly under the radar, and concerned parents must deal with a serious problem that can lead to physical injury, health problems, legal problems, and risk of HIV exposure.

Now parents have somewhere to turn for help. The non-profit Oregon Research Institute’s Center for Family & Adolescent Research (CFAR) has three programs offering free outpatient counseling by Ph.D- and Master-level therapists for teen substance users and their parents.

CFAR’s “Sequencing” program provides family and group therapy to teens (ages 13 to 17) who are using alcohol or drugs and experiencing depression.

The “AIMS” program is specifically designed for teens and young adults (ages 15 to 22) who are using methamphetamine and includes individual and family sessions.

Finally, “ASPEN” is a unique program for parents or guardians of teens (ages 15 to 20) who are using drugs but refusing to go to treatment. CFAR’s therapists assist parents with drug education, techniques for reinforcing sober behaviors at home, communication training, and coping with their own stress and frustration. The goals are to improve relationships within the family and convince the teen to accept treatment, and then the entire family is offered free outpatient therapy at CFAR.

At CFAR, parents are viewed as a valuable and knowledgeable resource for reaching this under-treated group of young people. Using drugs was the teen’s choice, but parents can play an invaluable part in the solution. Previous studies utilizing this therapy approach have helped hundreds of parents obtain treatment for individuals who otherwise might never have gotten the help they needed.

These free services are offered as part of treatment research studies funded by the National Institutes of Health, so participants must meet program criteria and be willing to participate in research assessments to help evaluate the program.
