Although residing in the Beaumont-Wilshire neighborhood since 1990, I didn’t attend my first BWNA meeting until well into the 2000s. Why? Two reasons: 1.) I had no idea what a neighborhood association did; and 2.) I was a busy guy, reluctant to take on added responsibilities. So what was it that finally motivated me to walk over to a BWNA General Meeting at Bethany Lutheran Church?--the enticement of a free meal, plus entertainment! It was April, and the meeting was linked up with the annual BWNA “Taste of Beaumont” event, which features a variety of cuisines from neighborhood restaurants and music performed by the Beaumont Middle School jazz band. With a healthy turnout of neighborhood residents, the BWNA leadership seized the opportunity, as it always does in April, to recruit new Board members and elect new officers. When it came time to ask for volunteers to fill Board vacancies, I surprised myself: Almost unexplainably, my hand slowly went up. I reckoned that the “BWNA spirit” had gotten the best of me. Fast-forward to today: What I’ve learned since then about the scope and importance of neighborhood associations in this city makes me regret waiting so long to raise my hand. Accordingly, I’d like to share a few of these insights with you.

To begin with, the more than 50 neighborhood associations in Portland play a central role in the political and social fabric of the city. As an Association President, I receive requests every week from governmental organizations, non-governmental organizations, politicians, business people, concerned citizens--you name it--requesting time at Board and General meetings to make presentations on a wide array of issues. The result: a 2-3 month waiting list. Thus, the neighborhood association is a primary conduit for the dissemination of information and introduction of programs at the local level.

Complementing BWNA’s activities is CNN. No, it has no connection with the cable network (as I mistakenly guessed during my first BWNA Board meeting!). Central NE Neighbors is an organization that brings together representatives from several NE neighborhoods (see map on page 8) to discuss common concerns, provide services and grant programs, and offer yet another opportunity for speakers to get their messages out to the residents of the community. At a recent CNN meeting, Commission Nick Fish--now in charge of Parks and Housing for the city, and the Council’s lone NE Portland resident--emphasized the importance he places in communicating with local residents through their respective neighborhood associations. Regarding BWNA, the Commissioner is eager for input about Wilshire Park.

As I’ve experienced it, BWNA has been both personally engaging and publicly beneficial, and I’m hoping it might be the right choice for you, too. The amount of time invested is up to each individual, and there is many activities to choose from. So, if the “spirit” moves you, just take a walk over to the church on the second Monday of the month or feel free to contact any board member from the list on the right. This is your year to get involved!

-Al Ellis
President of BWNA
