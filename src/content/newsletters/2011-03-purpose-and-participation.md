“What exactly do neighborhood associations do, and why should I care?” Although I haven’t heard it put quite that way (thankfully), I have run into my share of Portlanders who fit the implied description: never attended a neighborhood association meeting and know little or nothing about how neighborhood associations function. My reaction? Empathy. Fact is, I was that person a little over a decade ago--too busy with my teaching career, too busy raising two daughters, too busy caring for an ailing dad, etc., to allow myself to participate in BWNA--despite the fact that the meetings took place just across the street from my house, that they were held every other month, and, most ironic of all, that I, a social studies teacher by trade, had a professional interest in attending. Nonetheless, it took retirement to push me across the finish line. My wife (still a full-time teacher) and I made our BWNA debut at the annual Taste of Beaumont General Meeting one April, intent on enjoying the delicious cuisine samples, then staying to observe the business meeting afterwards--which mainly involved election of officers and selection of new Board members. What happened next was downright weird: When it seemed that nobody in the room would step forward to fill one of the vacant Board positions, I, inexplicably, raised my hand and volunteered--surprising my wife and myself. But that’s how it is for a lot of people: Once at the meeting, the neighborhood spirit takes over. One of the most important challenges for a neighborhood association president is to motivate residents to take an interest in participating--first as regular attendees at general meetings, second as volunteers in neighborhood events and projects, and lastly--vacancies permitting--as Board members. I found that serving on the Board is especially rewarding, because you have a say in setting the agenda, and you’re in the thick of the decision-making process. It’s also become apparent that one of the best ways to effect change on the local level--be it the need for diagonal parking spaces, traffic calming measures, or even cell tower monitoring--is through a neighborhood united in purpose and working collectively through its neighborhood association. At City Hall, testimony from individual residents is one thing, neighborhood associaton resolutions quite another. There’s political strength in numbers and unity, and the BW neighborhood has been a prime example of that. But back to the issue of attendance at meetings. It’s no secret that attendance at BWNA gatherings, with the notable exceptions of Taste of Beaumont and the National Night Out Picnic, has been disappointingly low. But we’re not the only ones. A couple months ago, neighborhood association presidents began exchanging e-mails concerning low turnouts at meetings and asking each other for advice on how to improve things. One frustrated president lamented that he had “long ago stopped fretting over low attendance,” attributing the problem to “a cultural attitude about how to spend one’s evening” rather than a failure of neighborhood association outreach efforts. However, most of the others cited successful ploys--from bringing homemade pies...to posting publicity signs around the neighborhood.... to social network promotions on Facebook. As for BWNA, I’m not sure we can deliver on the pies, but our Board members are putting up signs in their yards the week prior to a general meeting, and there’s an ongoing project designed to improve communication via our Web site (www.bwna.us) and on Facebook. Most of all, though, we’re counting on you, our loyal Newsletter readers, to fill the Fellowship Hall of Bethany Lutheran Church on those second Mondays of even-numbered months. So, now that you know what BWNA does and why it’s in your best interest to care, make it simple.

See you at Taste of Beaumont!
