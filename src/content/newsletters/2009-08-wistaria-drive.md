The neighborhood association and residents along Northeast Wistaria Drive are lobbying city officials to fix a dangerous section of the road that connects 42nd and 41st avenues.

Officials say there’s no money for a total reconstruction of the roadway, which is what it would take to fix serious design flaws. But even less-expensive fixes, such as blinking beacons, a readerboard sign and improvements to the intersections of Beaumont Street and Alameda Boulevard seem to be off the table for now.

The neighborhood association is considering hiring a traffic consultant to come up with possible solutions to present to the city.

Neighbors most recently approached the city’s Department of Transportation after two serious accidents this winter. In December, a car full of teenagers knocked over a utility pole, shutting power to the neighborhood for several hours. No one in the car was seriously hurt. In January, a family sedan hit a parked pickup on the street, seriously injuring the woman passenger in the front seat.

The problem is a “negative super elevation,” said Will Stevens, a program manager in the traffic operations office. As drivers head south from Alameda Boulevard, the road curves to the left and slopes. But the road’s design encourages cars to trail toward the curve. As a result, vehicles have crashed into parked cars, trees and even houses over the years.

The stretch of road links 41st and 42nd avenues between Knott Street and Alameda Boulevard and is part of a major north-south arterial. TriMet’s No. 75 bus line travels the street.

At a neighborhood meeting in June, Stevens was discouraging about ideas proposed by residents.

Flashing beacons would cost about $100,000 because the city would have to bring electricity to the traffic island. The city doesn’t have solar-powered beacons.

A temporary readerboard that displays speed might help, but is only effective while it’s there.

Because the street is part of a major emergency response route, it would not qualify for speed humps, Stevens said.

It might be possible to narrow 41st Avenue near Alameda and Beaumont to slow drivers as they approach the curve, Stevens said. The city has no money for such a project. But officials plan to make Alameda a bike boulevard using federal stimulus money, which might accomplish some of the same goals, Stevens said.

In early June, city crews installed larger warning directional arrows on a landscaped island. Stevens said he would contact the Police Bureau to encourage traffic officers to enforce speed limits, but couldn’t make any promises. The city has no other immediate improvements planned, but Stevens said that could change.

“We haven’t folded our tent and walked away from this,” he told neighbors.
