I live on Failing Street, and have often wondered about the street’s name. “Failing” comes from a civic-minded family that put its brand on Portland forty-five years after Lewis & Clark forged the trail west to Oregon.

The family patriarch was Josiah Failing. He began an exodus to Portland from New York in 1851. First, he and his sons traveled by ship to Panama, mule train to the Pacific, and another ship northward. His wife and remaining family members bravely followed two years later by ship around South America.

Once relocated, Josiah opened a general store to serve the new Portland community of a few hundred residents. He became mayor of the city two years after his arrival. Notably, he set his mind to forming a public school district because he believed it made sense to pay taxes to educate students for the working world and keep them out of trouble.

In 1864, Josiah handed over the business to his son, Henry.

Henry Failing was married to Emily Corbett. Together they had four children, three who survived to adulthood. Tragically, Emily herself died at a young age in 1870 leaving Henry to raise the children.

Apart from his busy home life, Henry learned from his father not only how to succeed in business, but to think about the livability of his city. He too served as mayor and supported education through the development of a public library system. He also was dedicated to improving the resources of the city. Even in the city’s early years, the need for clean water was a problem because of too many wells and a polluted Willamette River. Henry led the planning to get pure water down to the city from Mt. Hood.

If you have wondered about this odd-titled street, understand that Failing is very much an overachieving name—contrary to how it sounds.
