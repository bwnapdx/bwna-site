Harry Truman wasn’t one to shirk responsibilities. Message: When you’re president of anything, the buck stops with you know who! President Obama lives it every day. So do heads of corporations, and the list goes on. Ultimate accountability resides at the top, right? Well, perhaps not always.

In particular, I would argue that all-volunteer organizations—like neighborhood associations—are an exception to this maxim. Why? Because unlike chief executives in the political and business worlds, presidents of neighborhood associations are volunteers just like everyone else in their organizations. When the rubber hits the road in terms of “ultimate accountability,” remuneration is a non-factor in getting the job done. So what’s the motivation for volunteer leaders? Basically, altruistic values: a belief in the mission and a dedication to one’s commitment. People volunteer because they feel good about helping others, and in doing so, enhance their own lives.

But volunteering doesn’t have a monopoly on altruism. On the contrary, for most people, “charity begins at home”…which usually translates into family and profession coming first, volunteerism second. It’s a constant balancing act—what to take on, what to jettison in life. With volunteers free to opt in and opt out at will, neighborhood associations can never be totally assured of where the buck will stop, or if the buck will stop at all. The reality is that the buck stops with whoever volunteers to take charge at any particular time. And while the neighborhood association president might seem the most likely “last resort,” a sure thing it is not.

I’m reminded of the way past-BWNA President Albert Kaufman ran the Board meetings. Invariably, whenever a Board member would suggest some kind of new event, activity, or project for the Association to take on, Albert’s response would be the same: “Are you prepared to take the lead in organizing this?” The results were predictable. Board members thought twice before opening their mouths to make proposals—myself included. At the time, I thought Albert’s approach a bit too punitive for my liking, but now looking back from my perspective as President, I appreciate his pragmatism: He was determined not to become a “dropbox” for BWNA responsibilities.

A textbook example of the importance of shared responsibilities took place a couple months ago within the BWNA leadership. Board member extraordinaire Dan Johnson—who had been doing “triple duty” as Board member, Treasurer, and Newsletter Editor—reluctantly announced his resignation from all his BWNA positions, due to increasing professional and personal demands on his time. Suffice it to say, things looked grim. Filling a vacated Board position was challenging in and of itself, let alone finding replacements for Treasurer and Newsletter Editor, both of which require levels of expertise and attention to detail, and in the case of Editor, a major time commitment.

I feared the worst, especially the possible demise of the Newsletter, so crucial to BWNA’s communication with the community. But Dan had a parting “outside-the-box” suggestion: Why not “collectively” fill the positions? Long story short, Board members stepped up to the plate and volunteered to form a Newsletter Editor Committee, dividing the Editor’s duties among themselves. And Dan offered to stay on as “Bookkeeper,” which in turn motivated Board member Gwen MacPherson to become the new Treasurer.

We all came out ahead in sharing the load. And that’s the essence of community.
