What started last December with the public announcement by Clearwire of its desire to install a tower with transmitting equipment adjacent to a Beaumont-Wilshire (BW) residential area near the corner of 37th and Fremont remains a work in progress--with a series of twists and turns along the way that have caught the attention of Portland’s media, City Council, and other neighborhood associations facing similar projects. At issue are concerns about health (especially with respect to young children), noise generated by cooling equipment, and the eyesore of a tall tower rising amidst private residences--all of which may impact future property values of nearby homes. As a result, opposition to construction of such towers in residential areas has steadily strengthened and spread, and concerned BW residents--through their anti-cell tower organization “RespectPDX”--have led the way, raising enough money to challenge Clearwire’s project in court, should it come to that.

This has certainly not been a linear journey since last December--either for Clearwire or for Beaumont-Wilshire Neighborhood Association (BWNA). First there were two spirited meetings in January--both held at Beaumont Middle School--the first one a contentious neighborhood informational meeting with a Clearwire representative (required by the City) and the second a BWNA-initiated special meeting to consider resolutions of opposition to the project (the product of which has been printed in the last two editions of the BWNA Newsletter). But Clearwire had neglected to meet with BW’s two neighborhood business associations (Beaumont Business Association and the N.E. 42nd Avenue Business Association), as well as the Central Northeast Neighbors (CNN) Board of Directors (also required by the City). Clearwire complied in part by holding meetings with the two business associations, but it never did meet with the CNN Board. For that matter, the BWNA Board--which was supposed to be notified at the very beginning of the process--only learned about the project from neighbors living near the proposed construction site who had received fliers from Clearwire. And so it went, with BWNA, CNN, and the N.E. 42nd Business Association eventually publicly announcing their opposition to the project and Clearwire postponing submission of its application to the City for permission to proceed. The 70-page application was finally submitted in late May, which the City’s Office of Cable Communications and Franchise Management (OCCFM) eventually approved, despite BWNA’s written protest. The application was then sent for final approval to the City’s Bureau of Transportation, which requested more information from Clearwire before ruling. Meanwhile, RespectPDX has filed two legal appeals challenging OCCFM’s approvalwith primary emphasis on a City ordinance maintaining that Priority 4 residential sites (like the BW site) are to be last resorts for the construction of cell towers and that there be documented proof that alternative (Priority 1-3) sites are not available (which the appeal contends has not been satisfactorily demonstrated by Clearwire in it’s application).

One other matter complicates this thorny problem: The owners of Wilshire Market, who, at the outset, agreed to lease a portion of their property to Clearwire for installation of equipment--over heated opposition from local residents--no longer manage the store. Beginning in April, they leased the store to family friends. However, this new management--according to family member Hee Joo--now regrets the store’s connection with Clearwire’s project but can’t do anything about it, since their friends still own the store. Hee Joo told BWNA President Al Ellis over the phone that her family wishes to be good neighbors (e.g., donating bags of ice for August’s neighborhood picnic) but lamented that when the family leased the store last April, the owners assured them that cell tower opposition was localized and not indicative of attitudes throughout the neighborhood. Ellis responded that BWNA’s resolution in opposition to the project had already been in effect for three months by last April, but Hee Joo maintained that her family was not aware of it: “Our friends assured us that it (neighborhood opposition to the cell tower) would be ‘no big deal’--and we believed them.” One final note: While signs remain in residents’ yards advocating a boycott of Wilshire Market, neither BWNA nor RespectPDX has advocated a boycott. Hee Joo and family are reaching out to the neighborhood for understanding, saying the future of their business hangs in the balance. Will neighbors be accepting of Hee Joo’s explanation and sympathetic to her family’s desire to play a positive role in the neighborhood? Some say as the cell tower goes, so goes Wilshire Market...but only time will tell.
