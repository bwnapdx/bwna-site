Resolutions. Really? Resolutions are usually associated with the New Year as a commitment to a project or the reforming of a habit. Often, it is s a lifestyle change that attempts to rid the wrong and accomplish the right. Examples include getting in shape, donating to the poor, being more assertive, or becoming more environmentally responsible. But why do we wait until the New Year to make these changes?

During the Christian fasting period of Lent, the believer is asked to reflect through prayer, penitence, almsgiving and self-denial. During Judaism’s New Year, Rosh Hashanah, through the High Holidays and culminating in Yom Kippur (the Day of Atonement), one is to reflect upon one’s wrongdoings over the year and both seek and offer forgiveness. The concept, regardless of creed, is to reflect upon self-improvement annually. Thus, with our lives closely entwined to a calendar, we have created an identifiable annual event which we call New Year. This is the perfect opportunity for most people to reflect on their lives, embrace change and create resolutions.

For the 12% of you who actually achieve your New Year’s resolutions, I applaud you. For the rest of us, let’s get real. If you haven’t started to eat less and exercise more, you probably aren’t going to lose weight. Maybe this year we should resolve not to set resolutions and attempt instead to change 10% of our behavior rather than 100%? I encourage you just to start. Start saving money. Start exercising. Start spending more time with your family, and most importantly, stop making excuses.
